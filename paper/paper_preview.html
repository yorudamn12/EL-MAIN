Targeting or Difficulty? Controlled Tests of Weakness-Targeted
Synthetic Fine-Tuning for Small Language Models

RV College of Engineering, Bengaluru, India

Abstract

Methods that diagnose a language model's weaknesses and then fine-tune it on data built around them usually compare the targeted data with random or untargeted data. Targeted data is harder for the model by construction, so such comparisons cannot say whether a gain comes from aiming at the right skill or simply from training on harder examples. We test this directly for multi-step arithmetic word problems. A program generates every training and probe problem from a computation graph, so each problem's skill profile is exact. A Linear Logistic Test Model fitted on a fully crossed probe set estimates how much each property (number of steps, divisions, multiplications, magnitude, distractors) lowers the odds of a correct answer. We then compare, at an equal token budget and over three seeds, real-data training, untargeted generated data, data aimed at the weaknesses, and a control matched one-to-one to the targeted data on predicted difficulty. We run two targeting rules: aiming at the single strongest weakness, and spreading the budget over all significant weaknesses in proportion to their effect. On Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B, fine-tuned with QLoRA on one 8 GB laptop GPU, no targeted arm beats its difficulty-matched control on GSM8K or GSM-Symbolic: the four GSM8K differences lie between −0.023 and +0.011, the four GSM-Symbolic differences between −0.028 and +0.015, and every interval includes zero. One-sided tests rule out a targeting benefit larger than about 4.5 points on either benchmark when only problem sampling is considered, and about 7 points when the variation between training seeds is included. On held-out generated problems, single-weakness targeting is worse than its control (−0.158 for the smaller model, robust to seed variation; −0.047 for the larger), while targeting all weaknesses removes this deficit (for the smaller model +0.052 over its control, a gain that is not robust to seed variation). Every fine-tuned arm, including one trained only on real problems, loses 15–24 points on GSM8K relative to the untrained model; answer-level analysis traces this to imitation of the terse training solutions, which leads to more planning errors, and it is largest on the longest real problems. The results suggest that, for supervised fine-tuning on terse solutions, difficulty and coverage explain the benefit of generated data better than targeting does; whether targeting helps under a recipe that preserves general accuracy remains open.

Keywords: targeted data synthesis; difficulty-matched control; small language models; mathematical reasoning; item response theory; parameter-efficient fine-tuning

1  Introduction

Small open language models, with fewer than about two billion parameters, can be fine-tuned on a single consumer GPU. That makes them a natural test bed for focused, low-cost improvement: find out what the model gets wrong and train it on exactly that. Several recent methods follow this two-stage recipe, using clusters of evaluation rubrics [1], cognitive-diagnosis models over a skill matrix [2, 3], the concepts behind failed problems [4], failure-inducing instruction search [5] or clusters of the model's own error types [6], building on earlier work that augmented weak subgroups of classification data [7]. These studies report gains over untargeted or randomly drawn training data.

Such comparisons share a confound. Data chosen because the model fails on it is, almost by definition, harder for the model than data chosen at random, and harder training examples can be more useful regardless of which skill they exercise [8]. Some methods already restrict synthetic problems to a band of pass rates [4], which narrows the gap between arms, but the decisive comparison is missing: data that is aimed at the diagnosed weakness against data that is equally hard for a different reason. Without it, a targeting method cannot show that the targeting is what helps.

We build that comparison for multi-step arithmetic word problems, a domain where problem structure can be controlled exactly [9, 10]. Three ingredients make it possible.

  1. Exact skill profiles. A program generates each problem from a computation graph and computes its properties from that graph, instead of asking a language model to tag them.
  2. A statistical diagnosis. A Linear Logistic Test Model (LLTM) [11], fitted on a fully crossed probe set, estimates the effect of each property on the log-odds of a correct answer, with bootstrap intervals.
  3. A difficulty-matched control. Using the fitted model, every targeted problem is paired with another problem of the same predicted difficulty, so the arms differ in why the problems are hard but not in how hard they are.

We apply two targeting rules on two model sizes (Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B [12]) and add real-only and untargeted arms. Our contributions are:

  • a controlled protocol that separates the effect of targeting from the effect of difficulty, with exact skill profiles and a one-to-one difficulty match;
  • evidence from 42 fine-tuned models (four paper runs, three seeds per arm) that neither single-weakness nor multi-weakness targeting improves real-benchmark accuracy over a difficulty-matched control;
  • an analysis of why: single-factor targeting neglects co-occurring weaknesses, while targeting every significant factor makes the targeted and difficulty-matched data nearly the same, because under an additive difficulty model the factors are the difficulty;
  • an answer-level account of a large accuracy loss shared by every supervised arm, which we trace to imitation of terse training solutions rather than to the synthetic data or to answer extraction.

All experiments run on a single RTX 4060 laptop GPU with 8 GB of memory.

2  Related Work

Weakness-targeted data synthesis. Targeted Data Generation finds subgroups on which a classifier performs poorly and augments them with data written by a language model [7]. For generative models, CRAFT clusters rubric criteria into a capability tree and generates teacher-written training data for the weakest nodes [1]. CDS [2] and the work of Luo et al. [3] both draw on cognitive diagnosis: they estimate the mastery of knowledge components tagged by a language model and synthesise data for the weak components; Luo et al. additionally keep only candidates within an uncertainty band in an iterative self-training loop. ReverseGen trains a proposer to produce instructions on which the target model fails [5]. For mathematical reasoning, Self-Error-Instruct clusters a model's error types and synthesises examples per type [6], and SwS synthesises problems from the concepts of problems a model fails during reinforcement learning, keeping those it solves only part of the time [4]. These methods differ in diagnosis and generation; our study asks a question they leave open, namely whether targeting helps beyond the difficulty it brings along.

Synthetic and programmatic math data. Large synthetic corpora written by stronger models can lift small models substantially on GSM8K [13], and multi-step reasoning can be distilled into smaller models at some cost to their general ability [14]. Programmatically generated arithmetic data also helps smaller models, either as an intermediate fine-tuning stage or mixed into instruction data [15]. Graph-based generators give full control over problem structure: iGSM was used to study how models solve grade-school problems and where their planning fails [9], and GSM-Symbolic varies the numbers and clauses of GSM8K [16] templates to measure robustness [10]. Our generator follows the graph-based approach and uses it for diagnosis and for controlling the training data.

Item response theory for language models. The LLTM explains item difficulty as a weighted sum of item properties [11] and belongs to the family of explanatory item response models [17]. Item response models have been used to make NLP leaderboards more informative [18] and to estimate benchmark scores from small item subsets [19]. We use the LLTM in its explanatory role: because our probe items come from a crossed design, the fitted weights estimate the effect of changing one property while holding the others fixed.

Difficulty and data selection. Which examples to keep depends on how much data is available; with abundant data, keeping hard examples can be better than keeping easy ones [8]. Rejection-sampling methods under-represent hard problems unless more attempts are spent on them [20]. Our targeted arms draw problems in a band of predicted accuracy and weight them by p(1−p), the information a binary answer carries, so difficulty is controlled in every comparison.

Forgetting and the style of fine-tuning data. Narrow fine-tuning can erode earlier abilities [21]. Low-rank adapters [22, 23] reduce this but do not prevent it [24]. Supervised fine-tuning mostly shapes the format of answers [25], and for long reasoning traces the structure of the demonstrations matters more than the correctness of individual steps [26]. Training on text closer to the model's own distribution counters forgetting, whether by rewriting the targets in the model's words [27] or by mixing in the model's own generations [28]; on-policy reinforcement learning forgets less than supervised learning on external text [29] and generalises better [30]. These findings frame the accuracy cost we observe.

3  Method

3.1  Problems with exact skill profiles

Each problem is a directed acyclic graph whose leaves are the numbers stated in the text and whose internal nodes are the operations +,−,×,÷, evaluated in exact rational arithmetic. One of ten surface families (story types; eight used for training and two held out for evaluation) renders the graph as English sentences. From the graph we compute the feature vector qi: steps (number of operation nodes), log10_max (log10 of the largest intermediate value), n_mul and n_div (operation counts), n_carry (column carries and borrows), n_distractors (numeric sentences not used by the graph) and has_merge (whether two separate chains are combined). The features are exact for every generated problem.

3.2  Diagnosis with a Linear Logistic Test Model

The probe set crosses steps ∈{2,…,7}, digits per number ∈{1,…,4}, distractors ∈{0,1,2} and three operation profiles (additive, mixed, multiplicative), giving 216 cells. We draw four problems per cell (864 problems) and sample three answers per problem at temperature 0.7. With si correct answers out of ni, the model is

logit P(correcti) = θ − Σk qik ηk ,@(1)

where θ is the model's ability and ηk the cost, in log-odds, of one unit of factor k. We fit (θ,η) by maximum likelihood with a small ridge penalty on η and take 95% intervals from 500 bootstrap resamples of the items [31]. To compare factors with different ranges we use the range-normalised effect

Ek = ηk (Qk90 − Qk10),@(2)

where Qkp is the p-th percentile of factor k in a pool of 5,000 problems drawn from a GSM8K-like natural distribution. A factor is a weakness if its interval excludes zero; its threshold is its natural median.

3.3  Training arms

Every arm receives 600,000 whitespace tokens of training text. Generated problems are mixed with real GSM8K training problems at 3:1 (synthetic:real); the single-weakness targeted arm is also run at 9:1. All generated candidates come from one pool of 40,000 problems (untargeted problems from a separate natural-distribution pool), and p̂i denotes the accuracy predicted by Eq. (1).

  • real_only: GSM8K training problems only.
  • untargeted: generated problems from the natural distribution.
  • targeted (single weakness): problems above the threshold of the weakness with the largest Ek, with p̂i∈[0.3,0.7], drawn with weight p̂i(1−p̂i).
  • matched_control: problems at or below that threshold; each targeted problem is paired greedily, without replacement, with the unused one whose predicted logit is closest.
  • multi_targeted: the budget is split over all weaknesses with weights wk = Ek / Σj Ej; weakness k receives a quota of problems above its own threshold (largest-remainder rounding, largest weight first, each problem counted once), with the same band and weighting as above.
  • multi_matched_control: the same greedy nearest-logit matching, drawn from the rest of the pool plus one fresh pool of equal size, without any restriction on features. Because every significant factor is targeted, there is no “other reason” left to be hard; this control therefore holds difficulty fixed and leaves the mix of factors to chance.

Match quality is reported as the mean absolute logit difference and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic between the predicted accuracies of the two arms. When an arm would fall short of the token budget, the number of selected problems is sized by the shorter side and the pool is enlarged deterministically.

3.4  Error classification

Each wrong answer receives one label, checked in this order: format_error (no extractable answer), arithmetic_slip (a written equation is false), unverifiable (no equation to check), distractor_use (an operand equals an irrelevant number) and otherwise plan_error. Reference steps come from the graph for generated problems and from the calculator annotations for GSM8K. We checked these labels against an independent annotator on 300 sampled answers (Section 5.5). GSM-Symbolic has no step annotations, so we report only its accuracy.

4  Experimental Setup

Models and prompt. Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B [12], thinking mode off, with the step-by-step math instruction recommended on the Qwen3 model card and a boxed final answer. On a 200-problem development slice of GSM8K-train this prompt was the most accurate of the three we compared, for both models.

Training. QLoRA [23] with a 4-bit NF4 base, rank 16, α=32, dropout 0.05, learning rate 2×10−4, two epochs, effective batch 16, maximum length 512 tokens. Training solutions are terse: GSM8K reference solutions, and one line per computation step for generated problems. Every arm starts from the base model and is trained with seeds 0, 1 and 2.

Evaluation. Greedy decoding, 512 new tokens, bf16 weights. Each fine-tuned model answers the first 300 GSM8K test problems, 200 GSM-Symbolic problems spread over its 100 templates, and 135 generated probe problems from each of the training and held-out families. The untrained model answers a superset of these, so every comparison is paired on identical problems. We report means over seeds and 95% intervals from a paired bootstrap over problems, in which each problem's correctness is first averaged over the three seeds of an arm. This interval reflects the sampling of test problems but not the variation between training seeds; we therefore also report, as a sensitivity check, a two-level bootstrap that resamples the seeds of each arm and then the problems (Table 4). The primary outcome, targeted minus difficulty-matched control on GSM8K and GSM-Symbolic, was fixed before the first paper run; probe results and all other comparisons are secondary and are not corrected for multiple testing.

Protocol and runs. Configurations were fixed before each run, every run started from a clean code version, and only these runs are reported as results. The study consists of four runs: one single-weakness run per model (five arm settings × three seeds) and one multi-weakness run per model (two arms × three seeds). The multi-weakness runs reuse the baseline, diagnosis and the four single-weakness comparison arms of the same model, after checking that all training and evaluation settings are identical. A development study on held-out GSM8K training problems (Section 5.4) is reported as exploratory. Training took about 1.5 h and evaluation 15–26 min per model on the RTX 4060.

5  Results

5.1  Diagnosis

Table 1 lists the weaknesses. For both models the number of steps has the largest effect; division is second. The 0.6B model answers 36% of the probe problems correctly and the 1.7B model 64%. Accuracy falls from 73% at two steps to 14% at seven for 0.6B, and from 89% to 37% for 1.7B (Figure 1). Carries have no significant effect once the other factors are included, and magnitude matters only for the smaller model. McFadden's pseudo-R2 [32] is 0.28 and 0.25. The single-weakness target is therefore more than four steps for both models; the multi-weakness weights are 0.50/0.20/0.13/0.12/0.06 (0.6B: steps, divisions, magnitude, multiplications, distractors) and 0.49/0.29/0.14/0.09 (1.7B: steps, divisions, multiplications, distractors).

Result 1: no targeting benefit on real benchmarks. For both models and both targeting rules, the targeted arm and its difficulty-matched control are indistinguishable on GSM8K and GSM-Symbolic: the four GSM8K differences lie between −0.023 and +0.011, the four GSM-Symbolic differences between −0.028 and +0.015, and every interval includes zero (Figure 2). At the 3:1 mix, targeted arms are also indistinguishable from untargeted generated data and from real-only training on these benchmarks (for example, 0.6B targeted minus untargeted on GSM8K: −0.011 [−0.038, +0.017]). More synthetic data does not help either: the 9:1 targeted arm is never better than real-only training on the real benchmarks, and for 1.7B it is worse on GSM-Symbolic (−0.057 [−0.102, −0.013]).

How large a benefit can be excluded? A non-significant difference is not evidence of no effect, so we also ran two one-sided tests (TOST) on the same paired bootstrap, with 90% intervals (Table 5). We took three accuracy points as the smallest effect of interest. This margin was set after the main results were known, so for every comparison we also report the smallest margin it would pass. Equivalence within ±3 points is shown only for the 1.7B multi-weakness comparison on GSM8K; the other seven comparisons pass at margins of 3.0 to 5.7 points, so our data cannot exclude small effects in either direction. They do exclude a substantial benefit: the upper end of the 90% interval, a one-sided 95% bound on the gain from targeting, is at most +0.034 on GSM8K and +0.045 on GSM-Symbolic.

Variation between training seeds. The intervals above treat the three seeds of an arm as fixed. In the compared arms, the seeds differ by up to 3.6 points on GSM8K and up to 6.6 points on held-out probes (standard deviation of arm accuracy), so we repeated every primary comparison with a two-level bootstrap that also resamples the seeds (Table 4; thin lines in Figure 2). With three seeds this check is coarse, but it changes the reading in two places. No real-benchmark conclusion changes: all intervals still include zero, and the largest upper end is +0.059 on GSM8K and +0.068 on GSM-Symbolic, so a benefit of about seven points or more remains excluded. On held-out probes, the single-weakness deficit of 0.6B stays clearly below zero ([−0.247, −0.069]), but the deficit of 1.7B (−0.047) and the multi-weakness gain of 0.6B (+0.052) are no longer distinguishable from zero. We therefore treat these two probe differences as suggestive only.

Result 2: single-weakness targeting loses to its control on held-out generated problems. On problems from the two held-out families, the single-weakness arm is below its control for both models; for 0.6B it is below even on the slice it was aimed at (problems with more than four steps: 0.54 versus 0.65). The 0.6B deficit survives the seed-level check; the 1.7B deficit does not. With more synthetic data (9:1, for which no matched control was trained) the targeted arm reaches 0.704 (0.6B) and 0.894 (1.7B) on held-out probes, still below the 3:1 controls (0.760 and 0.919). All probe results, here and below, need one caveat: probes come from the same generator as the generated training data. Holding out two story families removes shared wording, but not the shared problem structure and answer format, so part of every probe gain may be learning of the generator's format rather than of the skill. The real benchmarks carry no such bias, which is why we treat them as the primary outcome.

Result 3: targeting all weaknesses removes this deficit. For 0.6B, multi-weakness targeting is far better than single-weakness targeting on generated problems (+0.168 held-out) and slightly better than its own control (+0.052, p=0.014 over problems, but not significant once seed variation is included). For 1.7B none of these differences is significant; generated probes are close to their ceiling for every arm (0.87–0.98).

5.3  Why targeting does not help: two mechanisms

Single-factor targeting neglects co-occurring weaknesses. Refitting the LLTM after training (0.6B) shows that the single-weakness arm lowered the cost of an extra step far more than any other arm (Δηsteps = −0.98, against +0.05 for untargeted and +0.23 for the matched control), so the intervention worked on its own terms. It barely changed the costs of multiplication and division (−0.01 and −0.14), which the other arms reduced substantially (−0.90 to −1.76). Long problems also contain multiplications and divisions, so the broader data produced higher accuracy even on long problems. Table 6 shows the same pattern per weakness: the single-weakness arm left division almost unsolved for 0.6B (0.07 to 0.35), while multi-weakness targeting lifted every weakness to 0.70–0.77. For 1.7B the post-training refit is poorly identified because trained models answer 87–97% of probes correctly, so we do not interpret its Δη.

Targeting all factors converges to matching difficulty. Under the additive model in Eq. (1), predicted difficulty is a weighted sum of exactly the factors that the multi-weakness rule targets. A control that matches predicted difficulty must therefore carry similar amounts of the same factors. The data confirm this. For 1.7B the share of problems above each threshold is nearly identical in the two arms (steps 0.73 versus 0.74, divisions 0.57 versus 0.53, multiplications 0.65 versus 0.59, distractors 0.74 versus 0.72); for 0.6B the arms differ mainly in the number of steps (0.53 versus 0.39 above threshold). The only significant difference between the multi-weakness arms, +0.052 for 0.6B on held-out probes, appears where the compositions differ most. Once every significant factor is targeted, “aimed at the weaknesses” and “equally hard” describe nearly the same data, and only the allocation across factors remains to be tested.

Generated long problems do not transfer to real long problems. Only 50 of the 300 GSM8K test problems (17%) need more than four calculator steps. On these, every fine-tuned arm loses the most: for 0.6B accuracy drops from 0.50 to 0.16–0.21, for 1.7B from 0.78 to 0.48–0.55, and the targeted arms are no better than the others (0.6B: targeted 0.17, matched control 0.19, multi-weakness 0.19 versus 0.21; descriptive, 50 problems). Gains on long generated problems did not carry over to long real problems, which depend more on reading and planning than on the arithmetic structure our generator controls.

5.4  The shared cost: imitation of terse solutions

Every fine-tuned arm loses 15–24 points on GSM8K relative to the untrained model (14–19 points on GSM-Symbolic), including real_only, which saw no generated data (−0.20 for 0.6B, −0.15 for 1.7B). Because the loss is shared by every arm, it does not bias the comparison between targeted and control data; it does, however, define the scope of our conclusions, which hold for supervised fine-tuning on terse solutions.

Exploratory ablation (not part of the paper runs). Before the main runs, a development study on 0.6B (200 held-out GSM8K training problems, one seed) found that the loss did not depend on the settings we varied: a four-times lower learning rate, a single epoch or a larger share of real data all left it between −0.20 and −0.24. A longer fixed solution template made it worse (−0.28), because the model repeated the template until the token limit. Only training on the base model's own verified solutions reduced it, to −0.115 with 65% and −0.08 with 85% model-written tokens, but this also removed most of the gain on generated problems (+0.26 fell to +0.10 and +0.08), consistent with self-distillation results on other tasks [27].

Answer-level analysis. To locate the loss we compared, answer by answer, each fine-tuned model with the untrained model on the same 300 GSM8K problems (Table 7). Answer extraction is not the cause: almost no answer from either model lacks an extractable final number. The fine-tuned models write about four lines instead of 25–30, in the format of the training solutions. The untrained model usually begins by listing every given quantity and then works through one step per heading; the fine-tuned models omit that list. On problems that changed from correct to wrong, most new errors are planning errors (a quantity left out, or a relation such as “ten times more than” misread), followed by arithmetic slips in short inline sums. For 0.6B the rate of false equations rises; for 1.7B it barely changes, so its loss is almost entirely in planning. The multi-weakness arms show the same pattern (plan errors 56–57% and arithmetic slips 23–28% of lost answers). These shares come from the automatic classifier; the label check below suggests that it counts too many slips and too few plan errors, so the slip shares are best read as upper bounds.

We read this as format imitation [25, 26]: supervised training on terse reference solutions replaces the longer reasoning format the model learned in post-training, and with it the habit of restating the givens that protected it from planning errors. The loss is largest on the longest real problems, where more facts must be tracked. Because all arms pay the same cost, comparisons between arms remain fair, but the cost outweighs any benefit that targeting could add with this training recipe.

5.5  How reliable are the automatic error labels?

To test the classifier, we drew 150 wrong answers of each untrained model from its diagnosis run, stratified by predicted label so that rare types were included, and had them relabelled blind. The annotator was a large language model (Claude Opus 5.5, Anthropic) that never saw the classifier's labels. It received the question, the gold answer and the response, applied the same five labels in the same order of precedence, and recomputed every written equation exactly with Python. The 300 answers were split into six batches of 50, each labelled in a separate session. The annotator is not a human, and agreement with it measures consistency with a second, independent reading, not ground truth.

The two labelings agreed on 46% of answers, Cohen's κ = 0.33 (0.38 for 0.6B, 0.27 for 1.7B), well below the 0.6 usually taken as substantial agreement. Reading the disagreements identified three causes:

  • Missed equations. The parser misses many calculations, for example ones written with “×” or inside bold text, and then labels the answer unverifiable; the annotator judged 49 of the 71 answers in that class to be plan errors.
  • Chained equalities. A line such as “17265 − 6469 − 3447 = 17265 − 9916 = 7349” is true, but it is parsed as a false equation. This is one reason why the annotator judged 23 of the 74 predicted slips to be plan errors.
  • Cut-off answers. When a response stops before its final answer, the classifier still reads its last number, whereas the annotator labels it a format error.

Treating unverifiable as a plan error raises κ to 0.42. Overall the annotator assigned plan_error to about twice as many answers as the classifier (149 versus 73 of 284) and arithmetic_slip to about half as many (36 versus 74).

After the check we corrected the parser for the first two causes (bold text, and chains whose middle part is an expression) and the extraction fault described next. On the same 300 answers the corrected classifier reaches κ = 0.39 (0.41 on the 284 answers that are really wrong; 0.52 for 0.6B, 0.27 for 1.7B). Most of the remaining disagreement comes from cut-off answers, which the annotator labels as format errors far more often than the classifier does (63 versus 17), and from slips, which the classifier still finds about twice as often (70 versus 36). All results in this paper were produced with the original classifier. The corrected version is in the repository for future runs.

The check also exposed an answer-extraction fault. Sixteen of the 300 sampled answers (15 from 1.7B) were in fact correct: their final \boxed{} value equals the gold answer, but the extractor gives priority to the GSM8K marker “####” and so read a Markdown step heading such as “#### 7.” as the answer. Rescoring every stored answer with \boxed{} first changes 25 of the 2,592 diagnosis answers of the untrained 1.7B model (1.0%) and one of the 0.6B model, and moves the untrained 1.7B accuracy by at most 0.5 points (GSM8K 0.815 to 0.818; training-family probes 0.625 to 0.630). No answer of any fine-tuned model changes, because those models do not write such headings. We report the numbers as logged.

None of the accuracy comparisons depend on the error labels. The labels affect only the error-type shares in Table 7. There, the check strengthens the main reading that planning errors dominate the lost answers, and the slip shares should be treated as upper bounds.

6  Discussion

Explaining each observed behaviour.

  • Targeted equals control on real benchmarks: the shared style cost dominates real-benchmark accuracy, real long problems are a small share of GSM8K, and gains on generated long problems do not transfer to them.
  • Single-weakness targeting loses on generated probes: it narrows coverage; the control and untargeted data exercise multiplication and division, which long problems also need.
  • Multi-weakness targeting repairs this for 0.6B: covering every weakness restores the missing skills; the small edge over its control (not robust to seed variation) coincides with its higher share of long problems.
  • No difference for 1.7B: generated probes are near the ceiling, and the two multi-weakness arms are almost the same data.
  • Every arm loses real accuracy, real_only included: supervised imitation of terse solutions, not the synthetic content, drives the loss.

Finding: under an additive difficulty model, targeting every weakness converges to matching difficulty. If difficulty is a weighted sum of factors, as in Eq. (1), then data aimed at all significant factors in proportion to their effects is hard for the same reasons that make any equally hard data hard. A difficulty-matched control drawn without restriction then carries a similar factor mix, and the two arms converge by construction. What remains for targeting to change is the allocation of the budget across factors, not the difficulty itself. Our data fit this reading: the only difference between the multi-weakness arms that is significant over problems (+0.052 for 0.6B on held-out probes, not significant once seed variation is included) appears where their factor mixes differ most (more long problems in the targeted arm), and for 1.7B, whose arms have almost the same mix and whose probes are near their ceiling, there is no difference. A direct test would build arms with equal predicted difficulty that differ only in factor mix.

Implications for targeted synthesis. Comparisons of targeted data with random data can overstate the value of targeting. In our controlled setting, the benefit of generated data is explained by difficulty and coverage rather than by aiming at diagnosed weaknesses. With an additive difficulty model, targeting all weaknesses and matching difficulty become almost the same operation, so the useful design space lies in how the budget is allocated across factors and in properties the difficulty model does not capture, such as language and planning demands of real problems.

Implications for training small models. With supervised fine-tuning on terse solutions, the general accuracy cost is several times larger than any effect of targeting. Training signals that keep the model's own reasoning style, such as self-generated or self-distilled targets with more attempts on hard problems [20, 27, 33], self-generated replay [28], or reinforcement learning with the generator as an exact reward [4, 29, 30], are the natural next step; with them the targeting question can be asked again without the shared cost.

7  Limitations

  • Match quality of the 1.7B single-weakness control was looser (KS 0.27–0.28) than in the other three comparisons (≤0.042), so that single comparison is the least clean. We report it unchanged rather than rerunning it; the 1.7B multi-weakness comparison, whose control is closely matched (KS ≤0.002), leads to the same conclusion.
  • Equal tokens, unequal problem counts. Arms are equalised on training tokens. Long problems use more tokens, so the single-weakness targeted arm contains 22–26% fewer distinct generated problems than its control (seed 0: 4,598 versus 5,930 for 0.6B, 3,857 versus 5,225 for 1.7B). This may add to its narrower coverage. The multi-weakness arms are balanced (5,321 versus 5,387 and 4,125 versus 4,104) and reach the same conclusion on the real benchmarks.
  • Ceiling effects. Fine-tuned 1.7B models answer 87–97% of generated probes, which limits how well arms can be separated on probes and makes post-training LLTM refits unreliable for that model.
  • Statistical power. With 300 GSM8K and 200 GSM-Symbolic problems per model, paired intervals are about ±3 points; effects of one to two points would not be detected, and equivalence within ±3 points is shown for only one of eight comparisons (Table 5). With three seeds per arm, training-seed variation is estimated coarsely, and including it widens the real-benchmark intervals to about ±5–6 points (Table 4); more seeds would narrow this. Re-evaluating the same adapters on the full GSM8K test set would roughly halve the interval width. The long-problem analysis uses only 50 problems and is descriptive.
  • Error labels agree only fairly with an independent LLM annotator (κ = 0.33, or 0.39 after the parser corrections; Section 5.5), and no human annotation was done. Error-type shares are therefore descriptive, and GSM-Symbolic error types are not reported. A small extraction fault inflates the untrained 1.7B model's error count by about one percentage point; it does not affect any fine-tuned model.
  • Scope. One task family, one model family, one training recipe (QLoRA supervised fine-tuning on terse solutions) and three seeds per arm. Qwen3's thinking mode was switched off throughout, so long-reasoning behaviour was not studied.
  • Synthetic text. Generated problems read less naturally than human-written ones, and probes share the generator with training data (see the caveat in the Results); human-written held-out probes would remove this doubt.

8  Conclusion

We tested whether weakness-targeted synthetic fine-tuning helps because it targets the right skill or because it supplies harder data. With exact skill profiles, an LLTM diagnosis and a one-to-one difficulty-matched control, two targeting rules on two Qwen3 models give a consistent answer: targeting did not improve real-benchmark accuracy over equally hard data. Aiming at a single weakness narrowed coverage and lost to its control on new generated problems (clearly so for the smaller model); aiming at all weaknesses repaired this but, by construction, became nearly the same as matching difficulty. Separately, every supervised arm lost 15–24 points of GSM8K accuracy by imitating terse solutions, so our conclusion is scoped to supervised fine-tuning on terse solutions. The first open question is whether targeting helps under a recipe that keeps general accuracy, for example solutions written by a stronger teacher and filtered by the generator's exact answers, or reinforcement learning with the generator as reward. We recommend that claims for targeted data synthesis be tested against difficulty-matched controls, together with a check of general accuracy.
